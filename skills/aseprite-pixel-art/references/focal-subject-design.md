# Focal subject design gates

Use this workflow for an important character or creature whose design must carry a scene or animation, especially when the subject, pose, or style is unfamiliar. It is intentionally heavier than the workflow for a small icon, incidental background figure, or already approved design.

## Define the art problem at delivery size

Record these before polishing:

- **Final footprint:** approximate width and height, in source pixels, the subject occupies in the delivered scene or sprite. A `256×144` proof does not establish that a character works at its intended `48×32` footprint.
- **View and action:** camera angle, facing direction, support, gaze, and the decisive pose or contact.
- **Identity references:** viewed evidence for anatomy, proportions, landmarks, and movement. Label observations separately from inference.
- **Style target:** viewed art demonstrating the intended contour rhythm, cluster size, palette behavior, detail density, and finish. Use it as direction, not a design to copy.
- **Simplification budget:** which identity and action cues must survive at the final footprint, and which details may disappear.

An anatomical photograph can answer how an otter's body connects; it does not answer how a sprite should distribute clusters or stylize its face. A style reference may support deliberate distortion, especially for fictional subjects, but departures from observed anatomy must be intentional, recorded, and coherent with the selected design. Keep both evidence roles explicit.

## Anchor identity before action

When the requested action is invented, anthropomorphic, or poorly represented by the identity references, first draw the subject in a neutral or canonical pose. Support it with a pose-matched observed reference, a user model sheet, or an explicitly invented construction informed by constituent references; label invented evidence. Establish construction and defining landmarks there. Then translate the same design into the requested action pose and compare the pair. Do not solve subject identity and a speculative action in one silhouette.

A style target should be reasonably comparable in topology, footprint, and intended finish. A partial target is valid—for example, a human study used only for cluster grouping—but record what it does not inform. It cannot silently stand in for a view- and subject-relevant form reference.

## Explore before committing

When the design is uncertain, make a compact silhouette sheet with roughly three materially different structural hypotheses at or near the final footprint. Three is a useful exploration target, not a universal quota. Vary meaningful decisions such as body rhythm, head-to-body ratio, limb separation, support, tail or costume mass, and negative spaces. Do not present the same construction with minor pose, ear, or color changes as different candidates.

Before selecting, compare each candidate against a short cue matrix: three to five observed identity cues, their proposed pixel translation, and the nearest likely confusion. If every candidate shares the same risky construction, reject the sheet rather than choosing the least weak option.

Judge the candidates in flat value first. Select one because its identity, gesture, and usable negative spaces best serve the brief. Preserve rejected candidates only as design evidence; do not polish all of them.

For the selected design, establish head, chest, pelvis, near and far limbs, support/contact, and major appendages before surface detail. Tiny or highly stylized subjects may merge some masses, but the merger must read as deliberate at delivery size rather than as unresolved construction.

## Pass distinct gates

Inspect a fresh native-size image at the actual delivery footprint. An integer enlargement may help diagnose pixels, but cannot replace this view.

1. **Structural silhouette:** does the flat shape communicate orientation, broad body class, balance, support, appendage rhythm, and useful negative spaces without an accidental lookalike dominating? This gate does not always require an exact species or character name.
2. **Semantic identity:** after a minimal block-in adds only the values or colors needed for defining internal landmarks—often two or three—can an uninformed viewer describe the intended kind of subject and facing direction at the semantic specificity required by the brief, without the name, costume, caption, or scene explanation? For an unfamiliar fictional subject, can they repeat the intended defining traits? An informed author self-review is provisional evidence, not a blind read.
3. **Construction and contact:** do the major masses connect coherently? Are support, balance, near/far limbs, joints, grip, and relevant contact believable within the chosen stylization?
4. **Pixel craft and style:** do contour steps, clusters, value grouping, palette, selective detail, and material cues look deliberate and match the chosen style target? Recognition alone cannot pass this gate.
5. **Action and story:** in the relevant pose or rough sequence, can a viewer understand what the subject is doing, what it affects, and the decisive change without reading the brief?

Apply only the gates relevant to the deliverable, but never merge their evidence. A still neutral character does not need a story gate; a focal animated actor does.

## Correct or restart deliberately

After a failed semantic identity read, make at most one bounded local correction when the structural silhouette and construction remain sound. This narrower identity rule takes precedence over general advice allowing one or two correction passes. State the observed confusion and the specific internal or contour cue being changed. Recheck at the final footprint.

If the structural silhouette itself fails—wrong body class, balance, support, or shared lookalike construction—restart the affected hypothesis or the whole sheet before adding identity colors. A semantic correction cannot rescue a failed structure.

If identity remains ambiguous, or if correction would require rebuilding several major masses, stop patching and choose or draw a new silhouette. Restart only the unapproved focal design; preserve approved assets and user edits. If the restarted silhouette fails the same gate again, stop the loop and request stronger reference, a changed art direction, or human selection. Likewise, do not repair poor construction with costume, texture, outline thickness, or extra colors. A successful restart is cheaper than multiplying a weak design across frames.

If the neutral identity anchor already passed but its action translation fails structure or semantics, restart only the action translation. Preserve the approved anchor unless new evidence shows that its underlying design is also wrong.

Every applicable focal-subject gate must pass. Structure, construction, and craft normally apply to focal characters and creatures; semantic identity applies at the specificity required by the brief. Contact-specific checks apply when the pose interacts with another object or surface. Rough assembly is allowed before these gates pass when it tests footprint, overlap, depth, or action, but do not polish the scene or multiply detailed frames from an unapproved design.

## Evidence record

Keep the record short and concrete:

- final footprint and view;
- identity and style references, including what was actually observed;
- candidate structural hypotheses, cue matrix, actual occupied bounds, and why one was selected;
- neutral identity anchor and requested action pose when the action was invented;
- reviewer type: uninformed viewer, informed reviewer, or provisional author self-review;
- actual caption-free identity description;
- each applicable structural, semantic, construction, craft, and action gate marked pass, fail, or unassessed;
- construction/contact critique;
- craft/style critique against the selected target;
- correction or restart decision;
- remaining uncertainty and next permitted production step.

Tool success, layer count, palette size, frame count, and a model's confidence score are production facts, not artistic acceptance.
