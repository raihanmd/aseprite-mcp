# Study the subject before drawing it

Use this for a new character, animal, distinctive manufactured object, or multi-part action where physical form matters. It is a short production step, not an anatomy encyclopedia or a requirement to imitate realism. The user's supplied references and intended style take priority.

## Build a usable reference card

Find enough views to understand the target view and the action, not merely attractive images of the same noun. Prefer the user's images, then first-party photographs, museum collections, maker diagrams, or footage. Actually view a source image or clip when claiming visual evidence; search snippets and model recollection are leads, not observation. If visual access is unavailable, say so, use attributable text only for what it can establish, and request a reference when likeness or mechanism is essential. Do not trace or copy a source image's composition into the deliverable. Save source links and a short description of what was observed, not unlicensed image copies.

Keep the card compact enough to use while drawing:

- **Identity and view:** which species, object variant, era, stylization, and camera angle? Do not combine incompatible variants accidentally.
- **Outer form:** the two or three proportion and contour relationships that make this subject recognizable at the target pixel size. Note useful negative spaces, not only surface details.
- **Construction:** for a body, head/chest/pelvis, limb joints, support, and direction of gaze; for an object, major pieces, openings, hinges, pivots, and what attaches to what.
- **Action:** what moves, where it originates, what it contacts, the path it follows, and the pose or state that proves the event. Check the source and destination in the same view.
- **Material and simplification:** which light/shadow or texture cues distinguish materials; which observed details are essential, exaggerated, or omitted on this grid.
- **Evidence:** URLs or user-provided reference names; label each claim as observed, inferred, or deliberately invented. Record a consequential uncertainty rather than silently filling it in.

Do not spend pixels on decorative texture until the large forms and connections survive a native-size preview. For a complex scene, make a small isolated silhouette/form study of each focal subject and important prop, then a rough assembled contact pose. If the animal reads only because of its costume, or a mechanism is understandable only after reading the brief, revise the study rather than multiplying polished frames. Compare the proof against the selected references, not just against the agent's own plan. An independent viewer's caption-free description is stronger evidence than the agent's self-check when available.

For a multi-asset project, retain the reference card with the working brief before producing the final components. If work is delegated, pass the card and any viewable user-provided references to the artist agent, or ask that agent to create and report its own card before drawing; a bare noun plus style words is not enough context. The lead agent should inspect the isolated proof and return concrete form feedback before the artist multiplies frames. Do not treat a completed card as proof that the resulting pixels match it. If primitive shapes cannot hold a defining contour, use more deliberate pixel clusters or report the tool limitation instead of burying the error under texture.

## Separate evidence roles and record final footprint

Record subject's approximate occupied width and height in source pixels in delivered asset, not merely canvas dimensions or enlarged proof size. Review the form at that footprint.

Separate **identity references** from a **style target**. Identity references establish form, connections, and motion. A viewed style target establishes contour rhythm, cluster scale, value grouping, palette behavior, and finish. One source may serve both roles only when it genuinely shows both. Record what was visible in source rather than treating title or surrounding text as an observation.

Prefer references showing requested view and action. When no single source does, label which source informs anatomy, which informs pose or contact, and which decisions remain invented. Do not splice incompatible views into one body without resolving their projection. A correct caption-free identity read is useful evidence, but it does not establish construction quality or artistic finish.

## Check reference fitness

Prefer a form reference matching the intended view and pose. If the requested action is invented or no pose-matched evidence exists, label that gap and make a neutral or canonical identity anchor before attempting the action. The anchor may use a pose-matched observed reference, a user model sheet, or an explicitly invented construction informed by constituent references. The anchor and action should visibly share the same construction; the action is a translation, not a fresh guess.

Record whether the style target is comparable in subject topology, source-pixel footprint, view, and finish. A partial target may guide only named aspects such as cluster grouping or palette behavior. Do not let a useful human shading study silently become a quadruped contour reference.

For important candidates, turn three to five observed cues into a compact matrix: observed cue, intended pixel translation, and likely confusion if mishandled. Use the matrix to compare alternatives before selection. Record actual occupied bounds rather than treating the maximum envelope as a box that must be filled.

## Example of the distinction

An unspecified "otter" is not one fixed silhouette. The [Smithsonian's river otter description](https://www.nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/north-american-river-otter) describes a long, streamlined body, short legs, a small head widening into the neck and shoulders, and a muscular tail. Its [Asian small-clawed otter account](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/news/meet-asian-small-clawed-otter-adorable-and-social-carnivore) emphasizes small ears, whiskers, a muscular tail, and lighter face/belly markings. Choose a visual interpretation and inspect photographs in the needed pose; do not treat these text facts as a viewed reference or force zoological realism into a cartoon. [Proko's animal-footage lesson](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/sketching-from-animal-footage) likewise treats observation of movement and basic structure as a foundation for stylization.

For a cuckoo clock, the bird's door and its relationship to the case are stronger action cues than extra wood grain. The [German Clock Museum's historical overview](https://blog.deutsches-uhrenmuseum.de/2017/07/20/ersten-kuckucksuhren-2/) notes that the little door distinguished early cuckoo clocks even when their cases varied. A user-requested winding key can be a deliberate fictional or spring-driven adaptation; make that choice visually coherent rather than silently assuming every clock uses the same mechanism. The museum also documents [spring-driven tabletop variants alongside weight-driven wall clocks](https://blog.deutsches-uhrenmuseum.de/2026/08/01/zwei-hasen-auf-einer-kuckucksuhr/).

These are examples of questions to resolve for a particular brief, not permanent otter or clock templates. For a potion bottle, vehicle, flower, instrument, or fictional creature, make the equivalent subject-specific card and visual proof.
